## longer term discussion

At the top of our website is "Empowering Unique Musical Journeys" and the first of our promises includes "tailoring personal journeys." Are we currently built to deliver that? I am not sure that we are. With only 12 units of electives out of 54 total degree units (allowing for different ensembles), the capacity for differences between BA students is limited.

Based on NASM accreditation, we are not required to do a full 5-year CAPR review for a few more years. However, our last review was 2020-2021. We made significant curricular changes at that time, and we now have several years to look back on. One example of a change would be the elective system. Previously, we had requirements and everything else (no specific electives), which was particularly significant at the UD level. Does having a two-tier elective system (music electives and free electives) serve our and our students' needs?

We are one of the larger BAs in CLASS, with 54 required units (the maximum allowable for BA under NASM). Numbers don't tell the entire story because configurations vary, with concentrations, tracks, etc. History's BA is larger but it is tied into teacher preparation. They are also one of the few departments that has grown. At the opposite end is Ethnic Studies, which is only 36 units (it also has the 5-concentration configuration). However, in all cases, students will graduate with a BA from Cal State East Bay.

## course descriptions

### MUS 306: Music and the Sacred: Ritual, Devotion, and Sound Across Cultures (Diversity Overlay)

A comparative survey of how cultures use sound to express devotion and transcendence: Gregorian chant, Sufi qawwali, Balinese gamelan ceremony, and Indigenous ceremonial song sit side by side. Students are pushed beyond cataloguing traditions to notice how their own background shapes what they hear as "sacred" in the first place. The throughline is comparative listening and cultural context, not theology.

### MUS 307: Forbidden Sounds: Music, Censorship, and Resistance (Social Justice Overlay)

A history of music that got banned, suppressed, or blacklisted: from outlawed instruments to apartheid-era protest songs to Soviet-era crackdowns. The course asks why certain sounds get treated as dangerous, and how musicians kept making them anyway. Case studies range from Nazi Germany's "Entartete Musik" campaign through the contemporary United States.

### MUS 308: Trance, Ritual, and the Music of Altered States (Diversity Overlay)

A comparative look at how musicians and communities use sound to induce or represent altered states: Siberian shamanic drumming, Sufi dhikr, Afro-diasporic spirit-possession traditions (Santería, Vodou), Pentecostal ecstatic worship, and popular music's own psychedelic borrowings (Alice Coltrane, the Beatles). The course closes by asking what it means for outsiders to listen into traditions built for participants, not audiences.
